How to Choose Safe Storage Materials for Your Collection

A practical guide to sleeves, boxes, holders, foam, cabinets, and display materials

A collector shopping for storage supplies soon encounters words such as archival, acid-free, museum quality, and photo safe. These labels sound reassuring, but they do not all have fixed meanings. The Library of Congress notes that “archival” has no standard definition and should not determine a purchase by itself.1

A better approach is to ask what the product is made from, what job it must perform, and how it will be used around the object.

This guide covers the materials immediately surrounding a collection: folders, sleeves, boxes, capsules, trays, foam, fabric, cabinetry, adhesives, barriers, and humidity-control products. Light, temperature, pests, handling, and room conditions also matter, but each is large enough to deserve separate treatment.

1. What should the storage material do?

Begin with the job rather than the product.

A storage material may need to:

  • support a weak, flexible, or irregularly shaped object;
  • protect it from dust and handling;
  • keep neighboring objects from rubbing or reacting with one another;
  • cushion it from movement;
  • block light or pollutants;
  • allow it to be viewed without removal;
  • help maintain a more stable environment.

The best material depends partly on which of these jobs matters most.

A clear sleeve allows a document or photograph to be viewed with less handling. An opaque folder offers better protection from light. A rigid capsule protects a coin from fingerprints and abrasion. A shaped foam support can keep a fragile object from resting on one small pressure point.

Chemical stability is only part of the answer. A chemically safe holder can still bend an object, scratch its surface, trap moisture, or make it difficult to remove without damage.

2. How can a storage material cause damage?

Most problems fall into four groups.

It releases something harmful

Wood, paint, adhesives, rubber, foam, and some plastics can release gases or particles as they age. Inside a small box, drawer, or display case, these substances may build up instead of dispersing into the room.

Wood and wood products can release organic acids. Fresh paints, caulks, and adhesives may release solvents or acids while they cure. Some foams become brittle and produce corrosive compounds as they deteriorate. Flexible polyvinyl chloride, usually called PVC, can lose oily softening agents and release acidic compounds as it ages.2

The smell of a new product is a useful warning, but the absence of an odor does not prove that it is safe.

Something transfers through contact

Dye, adhesive, oil, corrosion products, or chemicals added to plastic can move onto an object that touches them.

This can produce:

  • stains on paper or fabric;
  • sticky deposits on coins;
  • dye transfer;
  • tarnish or corrosion;
  • a photograph or painted surface adhering to its enclosure.

Direct contact deserves more scrutiny than a material used elsewhere in the room.

It holds moisture or pollutants in the wrong place

A sealed enclosure can protect an object from changes in the room. It can also trap moisture or concentrate chemicals released by something inside.

Tightness helps only after the materials inside the enclosure have been chosen carefully. A well-sealed case made with unstable foam, fresh paint, or acidic wood may expose the object to more concentrated pollutants than an open shelf would.2

It causes physical damage

A material does not need to react chemically to cause harm.

Damage can result from:

  • a rough or abrasive surface;
  • pressure concentrated on a fragile edge;
  • an enclosure that is too tight;
  • a heavy object placed on top of a weaker one;
  • fibers catching on a rough surface;
  • static lifting loose charcoal, pastel, pigment, or flaking paint;
  • repeated sliding in and out of a sleeve.

Fit, support, and ease of access matter as much as the name of the material.

3. Five questions to ask before buying

1. What is it made from?

Look for an exact material rather than a broad claim.

Useful descriptions include:

  • uncoated polyester, also called polyethylene terephthalate or PET;
  • polypropylene, or PP;
  • polyethylene, or PE;
  • lignin-free buffered paperboard;
  • cross-linked polyethylene foam;
  • powder-coated steel.

“Archival plastic” or “museum-quality board” does not provide enough information.

For coin holders, the Canadian Conservation Institute recommends using suppliers that clearly identify the material. When a seller cannot or will not disclose it, choose another product.8

2. What else has been added?

A product is rarely defined by its main ingredient alone. It may also contain:

  • plastic softeners;
  • surface coatings;
  • adhesives;
  • dyes;
  • fire retardants;
  • anti-static treatments;
  • ultraviolet absorbers;
  • recycled material;
  • printing inks.

This is why knowing that a sleeve is “polyester” is more useful than knowing nothing, but it is not the end of the inquiry. For valuable objects, prefer products made specifically for collection storage by suppliers that provide meaningful specifications.

3. Will it touch the object?

The closer the material is to the object, the higher the standard should be.

A questionable wooden shelf across the room presents a different risk from a wooden drawer surrounding a group of coins. A dyed fabric used outside a case presents less risk than the same fabric wrapped around a silver object.

Direct-contact materials should be smooth, non-staining, chemically stable, and appropriate for the object’s surface.

4. Will it be enclosed, and for how long?

A material suitable for temporary shipping may not be suitable for permanent storage. A product that performs adequately in an open room may cause trouble inside a small sealed case.

Consider:

  • the expected storage period;
  • the amount of material inside the enclosure;
  • whether the object and material touch;
  • how tightly the enclosure closes;
  • whether newly applied paint, adhesive, or sealant has fully cured.

Long contact inside a tight enclosure requires the greatest care.2

5. Can the arrangement be inspected and changed?

No storage material should disappear from attention once installed.

The object should be accessible enough to check for:

  • new stains or corrosion;
  • cloudy, warped, or sticky plastic;
  • crumbling or yellowed foam;
  • fading or dye transfer;
  • condensation;
  • odors;
  • pressure marks;
  • insects or debris.

Products age, formulations change, and objects may begin to deteriorate for reasons that were not visible when they were stored.

Paper: “acid-free” is only the beginning

Acidic paper and cardboard can discolor, weaken, and stain nearby objects. For most documents, maps, prints, books, currency, stamps, and comics, look for paper products described as both acid-free and lignin-free. Cotton rag or highly purified wood pulp are common suitable sources.13

Buffered paper contains a small alkaline reserve, usually added to help neutralize acids that develop later. It is a sound default for most ordinary paper collections.

It is not universal. Some photographic and printing processes are sensitive to alkaline materials. Cyanotypes, dye-transfer photographs, and some architectural reproductions are examples. When the process is unknown, a product made and tested for photographic storage is more dependable than choosing solely by pH.4

Plastic: ask for the polymer name

Uncoated PET polyester, polypropylene, and polyethylene are widely used for sleeves, envelopes, pages, and boxes.137

They are useful because they can be made without the softening agents found in flexible PVC. They also allow an item to be viewed without removal.

Plastic is not always the best enclosure. It can generate static, which makes it unsuitable for charcoal, pastel, flaking paint, and other loose surfaces. Photographs can stick to plastic in very humid conditions. A tight plastic sleeve may also make a brittle object difficult to remove safely.34

For photographs, look for products that meet the requirements of ISO 18902 and have passed the Photographic Activity Test, or PAT.45 PAT checks whether an enclosure material is likely to react with certain photographic images. Passing PAT alone does not establish that a product is suitable for every photograph or every use.6

Foam: choose for both chemistry and surface

Polyethylene and polypropylene foams are common choices for lining drawers, cushioning objects, and making shaped supports. Thick Ethafoam is polyethylene foam. Volara and Plastazote are cross-linked polyethylene foams.2

The exposed surface can still be too abrasive for a delicate object. A smooth interlayer of suitable tissue, fabric, polyester film, or nonwoven polyethylene may be needed.

Low-cost polyurethane foam gradually yellows, loses its resilience, becomes brittle, and may release corrosive products. It can be useful for short-term shipping, but it should not be treated as permanent storage material.2

Wood: location and enclosure matter

Wood is not equally dangerous in every situation. A wooden shelf in a large ventilated room is less concerning than a wooden drawer or display case surrounding an object for years.

Wood and wood products can release acetic and formic acids. Oak and cedar are among the more acidic species. Paper, lead, copper alloys, and some photographs are especially vulnerable.29

Better options include:

  • moving the object into an independent inner box or holder;
  • using powder-coated metal cabinetry;
  • lining or sealing the wood with a proven barrier;
  • covering wood with a stable laminate;
  • allowing suitable coatings to cure fully before introducing the collection.

A layer of cloth or ordinary paper does not provide a dependable vapor barrier. Plastic-laminated aluminum foil is used in museums because the metal layer blocks gases far more effectively.14

Adhesives: keep them off the object

Adhesive tape can stain, creep into porous material, become brittle, or fail completely. Removing old tape from paper, photographs, paint, leather, and other vulnerable surfaces can cause additional damage.2

Use adhesives for constructing boxes and mounts, not for fixing the collection object in place. Photo corners, folded paper supports, polyester strips, sewn ties, fitted trays, and shaped supports are usually easier to reverse.

Reversible starch pastes have legitimate uses in paper conservation, but that does not make household paste or a do-it-yourself hinge automatically safe for a valuable work.

5. Where does the type of collectible change the answer?

The same basic reasoning applies across a collection, but some materials need special attention.

Collection groupMain concerns
Paper, books, maps, currency, stamps, and printed materialUse clean, supportive, acid-free and lignin-free enclosures. Buffered paper is suitable for most ordinary paper. Avoid pressure, tight rolling, overfilled boxes, and plastic against loose or flaking media.3
Photographs and filmUse enclosures made for photographic storage and meeting the appropriate ISO requirements. Paper and plastic each have advantages. Avoid self-adhesive albums. Separate deteriorating film when possible because some older negatives release damaging gases as they age.456
Coins, medals, and other metalsAvoid PVC, acidic wood, sulfur-containing rubber, moisture, and bare-hand contact. Use holders whose composition is disclosed. Active corrosion, salt-contaminated metal, lead, and archaeological iron may require much drier or more specialized storage than a typical mixed collection.89
Textiles, leather, wood, and natural materialsSupport the object’s shape without compression. Use smooth, washed, non-staining materials. Watch for unstable dyes, snagging fibers, pests, and materials that are especially sensitive to alkaline paper or very dry conditions.
Glass, ceramics, stone, minerals, and fossilsPhysical support often matters more than chemical reaction. Prevent rolling, tipping, stacking, and pressure on repaired or projecting parts. Some specimens, including salt-bearing material and pyrite, have special humidity needs.
Plastics, rubber, painted objects, and mixed-material piecesIdentification matters because plastics age in very different ways. Isolate objects that become sticky, cracked, powdery, distorted, or strongly odorous. For mixed objects, protect the most vulnerable component and prevent damaging contact between unlike materials.11

These groups are starting points rather than rigid compartments. A framed photograph may contain paper, photographic layers, adhesive, wood, paint, glass, and metal. A medal may include silk ribbon, enamel, and a presentation case. The complete object has to be considered.

6. Build the complete storage arrangement

Good storage usually has several layers.

The primary enclosure

This is the sleeve, capsule, folder, tissue, or support touching the object. It needs the closest fit and the most dependable material.

The secondary enclosure

A box, tray, album, or drawer protects the primary enclosure and provides support, organization, and another barrier against dust, handling, and changes in the room.

The cabinet or shelf

The cabinet carries the weight and provides security. Powder-coated steel is a common museum choice. Wooden cabinetry deserves more caution, particularly when it is new, tightly enclosed, or used for metals and paper.89

The room

The room determines the temperature, humidity, light exposure, water risk, pests, and pollutants surrounding every inner layer. A good box can soften short-term changes, but it cannot make a hot attic, damp basement, or leaking closet safe.

When to use silica gel

Silica gel absorbs and releases moisture. In a suitably sealed enclosure, enough properly prepared gel can slow humidity changes or help maintain a dry environment for vulnerable metals.

It does not automatically “set” the humidity. Its effectiveness depends on:

  • how well the enclosure seals;
  • the amount and type of gel;
  • the starting moisture level;
  • the humidity outside the enclosure;
  • how often the case is opened;
  • whether the gel is monitored and reconditioned.

The Canadian Conservation Institute warns that adding silica gel to a leaky enclosure may make little difference. Improving the seal can be more effective.10

Do not add silica gel to every collection box as a precaution. First identify the problem being solved and decide how the result will be checked.

When to use pollutant absorbers

Activated carbon, zeolites, and other absorbent materials can reduce certain pollutants inside an enclosure. Their performance depends on the pollutant, enclosure tightness, quantity, air movement, and replacement schedule.

They are a secondary control. Removing the harmful material or isolating it behind an effective barrier is usually more dependable than leaving the source in place and trying to absorb everything it releases.

7. Three examples

Coins in an old wooden cabinet

A traditional coin cabinet may be attractive and historically interesting, but the wood and interior fabric may expose the coins to acids or sulfur compounds.

A practical improvement would be to:

  1. place each coin in a holder whose material is identified and free of PVC;
  2. keep the coins from touching felt, rubber, bare wood, or unknown padding;
  3. place a proven barrier between the cabinet wood and the storage area, or use independent trays that isolate the coins;
  4. inspect for new toning, haze, spots, or green residue;
  5. measure humidity before deciding whether a dry microclimate is needed.

There is no single humidity number that is ideal for every metal and corrosion condition.

Family photographs in a closet

Begin with the photographs rather than the box label.

Choose enclosures that meet photographic-storage requirements and have passed the relevant testing. Paper enclosures protect against light and are easy to label. Clear plastic sleeves reduce handling when photographs are viewed often. Plastic is best used where humidity is reasonably controlled.4

Place the sleeved photographs in a photo-safe box that fits them without crowding. Avoid sticky albums, rubber bands, pressure-sensitive tape, and loose packets of silica gel added without measurement.

A document in a wooden frame

Use acid-free, lignin-free mat and backing materials. Keep the document from touching the glazing. Attach it with a reversible or non-adhesive mounting method rather than household tape.

The wood frame can remain, but it should be separated from the paper package with a suitable barrier. The backing should protect against dust and physical damage. Hanging hardware should support the weight without placing stress on the document.

8. Final buying checklist

Before placing a product near a collection object, ask:

  • Is the material clearly identified?
  • Are coatings, adhesives, dyes, and other additions disclosed?
  • Is it suitable for direct contact, or only for outer construction?
  • Does it support the object without pressure, abrasion, or distortion?
  • Can the object be removed without bending or scraping it?
  • Will a tight enclosure trap anything harmful inside?
  • For photographs, does the complete product meet the appropriate photo-storage requirements?
  • For paper, is it acid-free and lignin-free, and is buffered or unbuffered stock appropriate?
  • For metals, is it free from PVC, acidic wood, sulfur-containing rubber, and other corrosion risks?
  • Can the object and enclosure be inspected periodically?
  • Is there a plan to replace foam, gel, absorbers, or other materials that lose effectiveness?
  • Does the supplier provide enough information to make a reasoned decision?

A safe storage arrangement does not depend on finding a single perfect material. It comes from matching the object with a suitable enclosure, using each material for a clear purpose, and checking the collection often enough to notice change.
